Molecular Gas–Liquid Symmetry in a Self-Open Closed System:
a New Vision of Phase Equilibria

In conventional thermodynamics, the equilibrium between liquid and gas phases is determined by chemical potentials, leading to unequal distributions of molecules between phases and a density hierarchy where liquids are generally denser than gases. In this work, we propose an alternative framework in which a closed system, defined as “open on itself,” exhibits a molecular symmetry: for a given constituent, the number of molecules in the gas phase equals that in the liquid phase. This assumption implies that the system is intrinsically half gas and half liquid, challenging the traditional view of phase coexistence. Within this model, the density of the gas phase can exceed that of the liquid phase, a result that diverges from classical equations of state such as Van der Waals or Peng–Robinson. The proposed approach, implemented in the EoSResol framework, opens new perspectives for the modeling of complex fluids, equilibrium studies, and potentially prebiotic chemistry, where unconventional phase behaviors may have played a role in molecular synthesis.

Introduction

The study of phase equilibria between liquid and gas has long relied on classical thermodynamic frameworks and equations of state. Since the pioneering work of Van der Waals, models have sought to capture the coexistence of phases by introducing intermolecular interactions and volume corrections. Later refinements, such as the Peng–Robinson and Soave–Redlich–Kwong equations, provided improved accuracy for engineering applications, particularly in hydrocarbon systems. In these approaches, the equilibrium distribution of molecules between liquid and gas phases is determined by the equality of chemical potentials, leading to unequal proportions of molecules in each phase. Moreover, the density hierarchy is preserved: liquids are consistently denser than gases under equilibrium conditions, except in the vicinity of the critical point where the distinction between phases vanishes.

Despite their success, classical equations of state remain constrained by these assumptions. They do not account for alternative symmetries or unconventional distributions of molecules between phases. In particular, the notion that a system could contain equal numbers of molecules in liquid and gas phases for a given constituent has not been explored within mainstream thermodynamics. Such a hypothesis challenges the conventional hierarchy of densities and opens the possibility that gas could, under certain conditions, be denser than liquid.

In this work, we introduce a novel framework, implemented in the EoSResol model, in which a closed system—defined as “open on itself”—exhibits molecular symmetry between phases. For each constituent, the number of molecules in the gas phase equals that in the liquid phase, implying that the system is intrinsically half gas and half liquid. This redefinition of equilibrium leads to unconventional predictions, including the inversion of density relationships between phases. By contrasting this approach with classical models, we aim to highlight its potential implications for the study of complex fluids, equilibrium thermodynamics, and prebiotic chemistry, where non-standard phase behaviors may have played a role in molecular synthesis.

Methodology / Model (EoSResol)

The proposed framework, referred to as EoSResol, redefines the equilibrium between liquid and gas phases by introducing a principle of molecular symmetry. Unlike classical equations of state, which determine phase coexistence through minimization of Gibbs free energy and equality of chemical potentials, EoSResol assumes that for each constituent in a closed system, the number of molecules in the gas phase equals that in the liquid phase. This leads to a system that is intrinsically half gas and half liquid.

The system is closed with respect to external exchanges, yet internally “open on itself,” allowing dynamic redistribution of molecules between phases.

Density Redefinition:
In EoSResol, density is reinterpreted as a function of molecular distribution and phase symmetry. This allows the gas phase to exhibit higher density than the liquid phase under certain conditions, breaking the conventional hierarchy.

Equation of State Formulation:
Equilibrium is defined by the constraint of equal molecular numbers across phases rather than by free energy minimization. This leads to unconventional predictions, such as inverted density relations and stable coexistence at non-classical conditions.

Implementation:
The framework is implemented in the EoSResol computational tool, available online. Numerical simulations demonstrate the coexistence of phases under the symmetry constraint, highlighting cases where gas density surpasses liquid density.

Results and Discussion

Symmetry and Coexistence:
EoSResol predicts a constant 50/50 distribution of molecules between liquid and gas phases, independent of external conditions. This contrasts with classical models, where proportions vary with pressure and temperature.

Density Inversion:
Simulations reveal conditions where gas density exceeds liquid density. This inversion is a direct consequence of the symmetry constraint and redefinition of density.

Comparison with Classical Models:
Van der Waals: unequal molecular distributions, preserved density hierarchy.
Peng–Robinson: improved accuracy but same assumptions.
EoSResol: equal molecular numbers, density inversion possible.

Implications:
Complex fluids: potential relevance in systems with strong interactions.
Prebiotic chemistry: unconventional phase behaviors may have influenced molecular synthesis.
Astrophysics: extreme conditions could favor density inversion.

Conclusion

This work introduces a novel framework for phase equilibrium, implemented in the EoSResol model, based on the principle of molecular symmetry between liquid and gas phases. By assuming that for each constituent the number of molecules in the gas phase equals that in the liquid phase, the system is intrinsically half gas and half liquid. This redefinition of equilibrium leads to unconventional predictions, including the possibility of gas being denser than liquid, a result that diverges fundamentally from classical thermodynamic models.

The implications of this approach are significant. It challenges the traditional hierarchy of densities and opens new perspectives for the modeling of complex fluids, prebiotic chemistry, and astrophysical systems. Future work will focus on experimental validation and numerical simulations to test the predictions of EoSResol against real systems. Ultimately, this symmetry-based framework invites a reconsideration of the foundations of phase equilibrium and offers a new lens through which to explore the diversity of fluid behaviors in nature and technology.
